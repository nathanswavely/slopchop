# Consent thesis holds, but revise trust and politics claims

The core of "Slop, Consent & the New Dividing Line" stands up: the idea that the AI-art "ick" is mostly about consent is now the mainstream language of the creator movement, so Nathan should present it as building on others' work rather than as a discovery. Karla Ortiz, Molly Crabapple, Ed Newton-Rex, Creative Commons and the Data Provenance Initiative have all made that argument since 2022–23. His genuinely new contributions are elsewhere. They are the **"influence follows reach" mechanism** (no source found that states it), **author consent / the byline as a promise** (the least-developed of his three consents), the **Closeted User persona** (absent from every published audience segmentation, yet backed by 48–57% concealment rates), a **codified builder's playbook**, and **per-string, self-reported copy provenance** (no equivalent tool found). Two claims need rewording before tomorrow. First, "trust loss comes from the gap, not the AI use" is contradicted by replicated experiments: **honest disclosure itself lowers trust** (13 experiments in Schilke & Reimann 2025; 16 experiments, N = 27,491, in Raj, Berg & Seamans 2026), even though being exposed by someone else costs more. Second, "predicted by occupation more than politics" is out of date. **Democrats became more AI-concerned than Republicans in 2026** (Pew: 56% vs 49%; liberal Democrats 63%), and **no major poll publishes AI attitudes by occupation**, so the occupational claim is a hypothesis, not a finding. The Lancaster newsletter story has no public trace and must be told as his own observation. His strongest critics are Lemley & Casey (training as "fair learning"), Cory Doctorow (a consent right would end up owned by labels and publishers), Creative Commons (binding consent would wreck the commons), Holly Herndon (agrees on consent, rejects the ick) and Altay & Gilardi (generic AI labels mislead). A Substack post is worth writing if it is told from the builder's side: disclosure has a cost, lying costs more, and here is how to disclose cheaply.

**How to read the flags.** Most research for this report came through search-engine summaries, because the research environment blocked pewresearch.org, newyorker.com, creativecommons.org, pluralistic.net, arxiv.org and several other sites. The notes' flags are kept throughout. **[secondary]** means the item was seen only in third-party coverage or a search summary. **[verify]** means check the primary source before quoting. **[unverified]** means background knowledge or a single weak source. Any quotation marked with these flags may not be exact wording; treat it as a paraphrase until checked. The verification checklist near the end lists what to check before going on stage.

Two more things are worth adding to the talk. The **gender gap** is at least as robust as any occupational effect that can actually be documented. Women are more likely to say AI is moving too fast (68% vs 58%) ([Pew, Oct 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/10/16/the-gender-gap-in-ai/)), and Data for Progress finds women net −10 vs men net +16 on favorability ([DFP](https://www.dataforprogress.org/blog/2026/2/27/public-opinion-on-artificial-intelligence-varies-widely-by-age-gender-race-and-frequency-of-use)). The **youth paradox** is also a stage-ready fact. Under-30s are the heaviest chatbot users (66%) ([Pew by age](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/06/17/how-opinions-and-use-of-ai-differ-by-age/)), yet 55% of them are now more concerned than excited, the first majority for that age group ([Pew, Aug 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/08/18/young-adults-in-the-us-are-increasingly-wary-of-ai-concerned-it-will-take-jobs/)).

The disclosure-penalty finding should be treated as material for the talk, not only as a correction. It explains why Closeted Users exist. Observers rate AI users as lazier and less competent ([Reif, Larrick & Soll, PNAS 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12088386/)), so hiding is the rational default. That makes "no negotiated etiquette" a structural trap and not just a gap waiting to be filled. A 2026 paper names the "perverse incentives for non-disclosure" outright ([ScienceDirect, 2026](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2949882126000551)) [secondary; authors unverified]. Nathan's pitch gets stronger once it admits this. SlopChop is not "disclosure is free." It is "disclosure is cheaper than exposure, and short, specific disclosure is cheapest." One study found one-line disclosures performed about the same as no disclosure, while *detailed* disclosures reduced trust and subscriptions ([arXiv 2601.09620](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.09620v1)) [secondary; sample size not retrieved]. In another, "AI-assisted" content was rated more credible than "AI-generated" content ([arXiv 2409.03500](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.03500)) [secondary].

## The consent argument has a three-year paper trail to cite

Claim 1's consent framing is the creator movement's own vocabulary, usually as "consent, credit, compensation." Ortiz testified to the Senate Judiciary IP Subcommittee on 12 July 2023 that AI developers train on human creators' work without asking, crediting or paying them ([Senate testimony PDF](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/2023-07-12_pm_-_testimony_-_ortiz.pdf)) [paraphrase; verify wording]. On NPR she said finding her work in LAION took away her ability to consent, and that she is open to licensing at fair rates ([NPR, Jul 2023](https://www.npr.org/2023/07/29/1190910319/an-artist-explains-why-marvels-use-of-ai-to-animate-a-sequence-is-worrying)) [paraphrase]. Crabapple's May 2023 CAIR open letter objected to images taken "without an artist's knowledge or consent" and grew past 4,000 signatures ([Artnet](https://news.artnet.com/art-world/open-letter-urges-publishers-not-to-use-ai-generated-illustrations-2294392)) [quote as reported by Artnet]. Newton-Rex's Statement on AI Training (22 Oct 2024) passed **31,000+ signatories**, including Thom Yorke, Kazuo Ishiguro and SAG-AFTRA ([British Copyright Council](https://www.britishcopyright.org/bcc-signs-global-ai-statement); [TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/computing/artificial-intelligence/kevin-bacon-julianne-moore-thom-yorke-and-10k-creators-sign-warning-against-ai-use-of-their-work)) [statement wording secondary; verify at aitrainingstatement.org]. In music, the Artist Rights Alliance letter (Apr 2024, 200+ artists) ([MusicTech](https://musictech.com/news/industry/billie-eilish-jon-bon-jovi-open-letter-ai-music)) and the 1,000-artist silent album *Is This What We Want?* (Feb 2025) ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/musicians-silent-album-protest-uk-proposed-ai-copyright-law-change-rcna193660)) helped kill the UK's proposed opt-out. The government's 18 Mar 2026 report dropped it after only about **3%** of ~11,500 consultation responses backed opt-out ([Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/articles/uk-copyright-and-ai-report-the-opt-out-is-dead-but-what-comes-next/)). That is the clearest policy evidence that opt-in consent has become the public default.

On reciprocity, which is the "influence follows reach" territory, the closest prior statement comes from Creative Commons. Its April 2025 post warns that when entitlement leads to misuse, the social contract breaks and reciprocation evaporates ([CC, "Reciprocity in the Age of AI"](https://creativecommons.org/2025/04/02/reciprocity-in-the-age-of-ai/)) [search-summary wording; verify]. In June 2025 CC billed its CC signals framework as "a new social contract for the age of AI" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/06/25/creative-commons-debuts-cc-signals-a-framework-for-an-open-ai-ecosystem)). The framework was still "in the pilot stages" as of December 2025 ([CC](https://creativecommons.org/2025/12/19/what-we-built-together-in-2025/)) [secondary]. Jaron Lanier's data-dignity work is the intellectual precedent for "credit should flow back." His 2023 New Yorker essay "There Is No A.I." reframes models as a social collaboration whose behavior could be explained through a "provenance calculation" ([Berkeley CDSS talk abstract](https://cdss.berkeley.edu/video/data-dignity-and-inversion-ai-jaron-lanier)) [essay not re-fetched]. Justine Tunney argues that training pipelines strip authorship metadata ([justine.lol](https://justine.lol/history/)). Open Future argues that individual opt-outs can never restore reciprocity because no single work measurably affects a model ([Open Future](https://openfuture.eu/blog/ai-the-commons-and-the-limits-of-copyright/)).

The empirical anchor for "the contract was stretched and people are pulling back" is **Longpre et al., "Consent in Crisis"** (NeurIPS 2024). In one year, robots.txt restrictions covered about **5% of all C4 tokens and 25–28% of tokens from the most critical sources**, and Terms of Service restricted about **45% of C4** ([arXiv 2407.14933](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.14933)). For the three-consents structure, Nathan should cite the same group's "Data Authenticity, Consent, & Provenance for AI are all broken" ([arXiv 2404.12691](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.12691)). That paper links source consent to authenticity and provenance, which is close enough to his framework that leaving it out would look like reinventing it.

On the code side (claim 2), the story is about conditional consent, now being litigated as contract law. Stack Overflow's May 2024 OpenAI deal caused a revolt. Contributors who deleted answers in protest had their edits reverted and were suspended ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2024/05/09/stack_overflow_banning_users_who/)). In **Doe v. GitHub**, the Ninth Circuit on 16 Sep 2026 affirmed dismissal of the DMCA attribution-stripping theory. It held that Copilot produces new works that "never contained" the copyright notice, so nothing was removed. The **open-source-license breach-of-contract claims survive** in district court ([Authors Alliance](https://www.authorsalliance.org/2026/09/23/resolving-an-interlocutory-appeal-ninth-circuit-affirms-dismissal-of-section-1202-dmca-claims-in-ongoing-doe-v-github-litigation/); [Fenwick](https://www.fenwick.com/insights/publications/nothing-remove-ninth-circuit-holds-copilot-creates-new-code-not-stripped)) [via search summaries]. That makes MIT attribution and GPL share-alike the live legal test of Nathan's spectrum. FSF called Copilot "unacceptable and unjust" in 2021 ([InfoWorld](https://www.infoworld.com/article/3627319/github-copilot-is-unacceptable-and-unjust-says-free-software-foundation.html)), and SFC launched "Give Up GitHub" in 2022 ([SFC blog](https://sfconservancy.org/blog/2022/feb/03/github-copilot-copyleft-gpl/)). The "why do we celebrate Copilot but reject AI art" asymmetry is a well-worn Hacker News debate ([HN 43806291](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=43806291)), so the spectrum idea needs a fresh twist. The 2025–26 twist is that open-source maintainers are now revolting against AI slop in their own commons. Gentoo and NetBSD ban AI contributions, curl ended its bug bounty, and Ghostty moved from requiring disclosure to gatekeeping ([Open {re}Source](https://openresource.dev/articles/ai-slop-contribution-policy); [RedMonk](https://redmonk.com/kholterhoff/2026/02/03/ai-slopageddon-and-the-oss-maintainers/)).

The audience-and-author side also has prior art. Ted Chiang's intention argument is an **authenticity** objection, not a consent objection. He said in a 2025 Princeton lecture: "If we think of art as a concentrated form of intention, then generative AI is a way of diluting intention" ([Princeton AI Lab](https://ai.princeton.edu/news/2025/ted-chiang-explores-%E2%80%9Cincompatibilities%E2%80%9D-between-generative-ai-and-art)). His New Yorker essay "Why A.I. Isn't Going to Make Art" (Aug 2024) was not re-fetched, so paraphrase it. Chiang is useful because he shows the "ick" has at least two parts, and a fully licensed model would still fail his test. Simon Willison's May 2024 definition of slop as AI content that is unrequested and unreviewed, with "don't publish slop" as a personal ethics baseline ([simonwillison.net](https://simonwillison.net/2024/may/8/slop)), is the direct ancestor of SlopChop's review-every-string workflow. Nathan should credit him. The "Closeted User" persona sits on top of a large body of data. **57%** of employees across 47 countries admit hiding AI use ([KPMG/Melbourne via Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/next/2025/05/05/almost-half-of-workers-that-use-ai-on-the-job-dont-trust-it-new-survey-shows)). **52%** of AI users are reluctant to admit using it for important tasks ([Microsoft Work Trend Index 2024](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/ai-at-work-is-here-now-comes-the-hard-part)). **48%** are uncomfortable telling a manager ([Slack Workforce Index](https://www.techrepublic.com/article/slack-workforce-study-generative-ai/)). For "people want to know," Pew found **76%** say it is extremely or very important to be able to tell AI content from human content, while 53% doubt they could ([Pew topline](https://alpha.pewresearch.org/pewresearch-org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2025/09/PS_2025.9.17_AI-and-its-impact_topline.pdf)). This is an importance item, not a labeling item, so don't call it "76% want labels."

## Six serious people who would argue with him, and their best arguments

**Mark Lemley & Bryan Casey ("Fair Learning," Texas Law Review 2021)** offer the strongest legal-academic objection. Copyright has never given authors control over who *learns from* their work, only over reproduction of expression. Facts, ideas and style are deliberately left free, so a consent right over learning would be a *new* right, not the restoration of an old one ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3528447); [Texas L. Rev. PDF](https://texaslawreview.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Lemley.Printer.pdf)) [thesis paraphrased]. This hits "influence follows reach" directly. A critic would say there was never a contract, only a legal default that ideas and style are free, under which every artist borrowed without asking. Lemley's later co-authored "Foundation Models and Fair Use" concedes that generative outputs resembling training data are a real problem ([Stanford Law](https://law.stanford.edu/press/ai-training-data-dilemma-legal-experts-argue-for-fair-use)). That concession is where Nathan's "competes with the source" has the most force, but it is a market-harm argument, not a consent argument. The courts lean the same way. Judge Alsup held that training on lawfully acquired books was fair use, while piracy was not, and the case ended in a **$1.5B settlement** finally approved 20 July 2026 ([Authors Guild](https://authorsguild.org/news/court-grants-final-approval-anthropic-copyright-settlement/)). Judge Chhabria ruled for Meta but wrote that "market dilution" would often make unpermitted training illegal ([Authors Alliance](https://www.authorsalliance.org/2025/06/26/meta-wins-on-fair-use-for-now-but-court-leaves-door-open-for-market-dilution/)). The UK *Getty* court held that model weights do not store copies ([AIPPI](https://www.aippi.org/news/getty-images-v-stability-ai-uk-high-court-finds-no-secondary-copyright-infringement-in-landmark-genai-ruling/)). The US DOJ filed in support of fair use in *NYT v. OpenAI* on 1 Sep 2026 ([AI Lawsuit Tracker](https://ailawsuittracker.com/cases/new-york-times-v-openai/)). The one appellate loss for an AI firm, *Thomson Reuters v. Ross* (3d Cir., 29 Sep 2026), involved a non-generative competing product ([LawNext](https://www.lawnext.com/2026/09/3rd-circuit-rules-for-thomson-reuters-in-its-copyright-fight-against-legal-research-startup-ross.html)). The honest summary is that **the law is unsettled, AI firms are winning on training and losing on piracy and direct competition**, and courts frame harm as substitution, not lack of permission.

**Cory Doctorow (with Rebecca Giblin, *Chokepoint Capitalism*)** accepts the diagnosis but rejects the remedy. Copyright can be assigned, so any new "training right" becomes one more clause that labels, publishers and studios make artists sign away. His Locus metaphor: giving a bullied kid more lunch money doesn't get him lunch ([Locus, Nov 2022](https://locusmag.com/2022/11/commentary-by-cory-doctorow-the-swerve)) [quote as reproduced in search results; verify]. His alternatives are sectoral bargaining (as in the WGA strike) and the non-copyrightability of AI output ([Pluralistic, Feb 2023 via LinkedIn share](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/robjdjones_pluralistic-copyright-wont-solve-creators-activity-7029931651040256000-DkbO); [Pluralistic, Mar 2025](https://pluralistic.net/2025/03/18/asbestos-in-the-walls/)) [summaries only; page unreachable]. Nathan's own evidence supports this critique. Music's consent was obtained from *rightsholders*. UMG–Udio and WMG–Suno settled into licensing deals, which Virginie Berger calls "launch, train, settle" ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/virginieberger/2025/12/18/launch-train-settle-how-suno-and-udios-licensing-deals-made-copyright-infringement-profitable/)), and the Bartz money goes to "copyright owners." Doctorow's sharpest question to Nathan: *who would actually hold the consent right?* The talk is stronger if it adds that fourth party, rightsholder vs. creator, to the source-consent discussion.

**EFF** adds the incumbency argument. Licensing requirements would favor firms that can afford a $1.5B settlement and would shut out open-source and academic models. EFF says the real protection for artists is labor law, not copyright ([EFF two-pager, Jul 2023](https://kittens.eff.org/files/2023/07/24/2023.07.14_ai_two_pager.pdf)). Open Future makes a similar point about who bears the cost of the "Consent in Crisis" restrictions: small firms and researchers ([Open Future](https://openfuture.eu/note/consent-in-crisis-the-rapid-decline-of-the-ai-data-commons/)). **Creative Commons** is both critic and ally. It warns that restrictive licenses may not block training and "may actually end up preventing the kind of sharing you want" ([CC, May 2025](https://creativecommons.org/2025/05/15/understa)), so it builds non-binding preference signals instead. That is close to Nathan's voluntary-disclosure approach, and he should say so.

**Holly Herndon & Mat Dryhurst** are his best "smart disagreement." They agree consent matters and built the infrastructure for it: Spawning's Have I Been Trained and Do Not Train registry, Holly+ under shared governance, and the Spawn model trained on a paid, consenting vocal ensemble ([Sounding Future](https://soundingfuture.com/en/article/holly-holly-herndons-digital-ai-voice-twin); [ArtReview](https://artreview.com/ai-art-and-the-problem-of-consent/)). But they treat AI as a medium to shape through protocol, not an ick to avoid. Grimes's 50% royalty offer ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/04/26/grimes-ai-generated-drake-music-legal-issues)) models a permissionless approach with revenue sharing, which answers "no credit, no audience" through the market. Zhou & Lee's study of 4M+ artworks found AI adopters produced about 25% more output and that rewards became *less* concentrated, though average novelty fell ([PNAS Nexus 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10914360)). That is the evidence for "AI widens who gets to make things."

**Fabrizio Gilardi and Sacha Altay** (with David Rand's group) argue that process labels misinform. Across N = 4,976, an "AI-generated" label lowered perceived accuracy by 2.66 points *whether the headline was true or false, human or AI*, because people read "AI" as "unsupervised" ([PNAS Nexus 2024](https://academic.oup.com/pnasnexus/article/3/10/pgae403/7795946)). Wittenberg, Rand et al. argue platforms must choose between labeling *process* and labeling *harm* ([MediaWell](https://mediawell.ssrc.org/?p=513439)). This is a direct challenge to SlopChop, and Nathan has a ready reply. SlopChop's per-string, three-way scheme is a specific process label. It addresses the "AI = fully automated" misreading that Altay & Gilardi identify, because "ai-revised" and "ai-fine" say what the AI actually did. One accessibility argument should be raised before a critic raises it: detectors misclassified non-native English essays as AI about **61%** of the time ([Liang et al., arXiv 2304.02819](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02819v1)), so enforcing disclosure by detection penalizes the wrong people. That supports SlopChop's choice not to be a detector.

The pattern is clear. The *moral premise* (consent) is borrowed, and Nathan should say so openly. The *mechanisms* (reach-gated reciprocity, the byline as a promise, the closeted layer, the audience-facing line) and the *instrument* (SlopChop) are his. "Not found" is weaker than "does not exist." The notes name Robin Sloan's essay "Is it okay?" (~Feb 2025) as the biggest risk to the originality of "influence follows reach." It could not be retrieved, and its content is unverified, so Nathan should read it before claiming novelty in print.

The builder cases support his playbook with real outcomes. Procreate's specific pledge never to add generative AI drew wide praise from artists ([TechCrunch, Aug 2024](https://techcrunch.com/2024/08/19/procreate-takes-a-stand-against-generative-ai-vows-to-never-incorporate-the-tech-into-its-products)). Cara grew from about 40k to 650k users in a week on an anti-AI stance ([Wikipedia: Cara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cara_(app))). Ironically, Cara enforces that stance with AI detection, which is a neat illustration that "AI vs. no AI" is not the line that matters. Adobe defused its 2024 ToS crisis with a plain-language "won't" on training ([CG Channel](https://www.cgchannel.com/2024/06/adobe-updates-its-terms-of-use-following-artist-backlash/)). The cases that went badly involved AI on audience-facing surfaces. Wacom blamed a third party for its AI marketing image ([PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2024/01/10/wacom-enraged-customers-by-using-ai-art-but-says-its-not-to-blame)). WotC said AI "crept into" a vendor's work through Photoshop ([TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/gaming/wizards-of-the-coast-admits-that-we-made-a-mistake-after-ai-art-confusion)). Vendor excuses didn't protect either brand. One caution cuts against the strong version of claim 7: open, honest use also drew backlash. Coca-Cola's AI holiday ads ([Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/creativity/how-coca-colas-ai-holiday-ad-went-from-praise-to-rage/)) and Duolingo's "AI-first" memo were both public. Neither was commercially costly, though: TechCrunch reported Duolingo's backlash "didn't even matter" to its financials ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/08/07/the-backlash-against-duolingo-going-ai-first-didnt-even-matter/embed/)). For local communities, the documented pattern is fair and festival posters: Oakland First Fridays ([SF Chronicle](https://www.sfchronicle.com/entertainment/article/oakland-first-fridays-ai-art-20252866.php)), Bozeman ([Cowboy State Daily](https://cowboystatedaily.com/2025/11/29/bozemans-christmas-poster-sparks-fierce-ai-art-backlash/)) and the Ohio State Fair ([NBC4](https://www.nbc4i.com/news/local-news/central-ohio-news/ai-poster-causes-controversy-in-ohio-state-fair-contest/)). These are the closest public analogues to the Lancaster story. No local-newsletter case was found.

## Conclusion

The research changes the talk's center of gravity. The strongest material is not that the ick is about consent, which is three years old and belongs to Ortiz and Newton-Rex. It is the finding that **honesty is penalized, hiding is rational, and exposure is catastrophic**, which explains why there is no etiquette yet and why it won't appear on its own. That reframes SlopChop. It is not a trust machine. It lowers the cost of telling the truth: the claims are specific enough to be worth making and short enough to avoid the detailed-disclosure penalty, and its granularity makes a lie costly to tell. It also turns the politics slide from a description into a deadline. AI scrambled coalitions in 2025, but it is sorting along partisan lines in 2026, so cross-partisan norms have to be set soon or not at all.

Two of Nathan's ideas look strong enough to carry an essay on their own, and he should own them in print: **reach-gated reciprocity** (the old bargain paid artists in audience, and training pays nothing) and the **byline as a promise** (author consent). Neither appeared in the literature the research covered. Both survive the best counterarguments, because they don't depend on a new legal right that Lemley or Doctorow could knock down. They are claims about norms, and norms are exactly what nobody has negotiated yet.
