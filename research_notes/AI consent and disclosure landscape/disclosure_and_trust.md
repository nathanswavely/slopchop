# AI Disclosure, Trust, and Misrepresentation: Empirical Evidence and Cases (as of Oct 2026)

Research note: several primary-source hosts (arxiv.org, pubmed, par.nsf.gov) could not be fetched from this environment. Many details below come from search-result summaries of abstracts, university press releases, and trade press rather than full texts. Each item that relies on a secondary source says so. ## Q1. The "AI disclosure penalty" / transparency dilemma, and being caught vs. self-disclosing

### Takeaway
Strong, replicated evidence shows that saying you used AI lowers trust and evaluations. That is a real cost to honesty. The best study (Schilke & Reimann 2025, 13 experiments) also finds that being exposed by a third party for undisclosed AI use hurts trust more than disclosing it yourself. This partly supports the speaker's "the gap is worse than the use" thesis, but it contradicts any claim that disclosure costs nothing. Its cost is smaller than the cost of getting caught.

### Cited Findings
- **Schilke & Reimann (2025), "The transparency dilemma: How AI disclosure erodes trust," *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* vol. 188, article 104405 (online April 2025).** Across 13 experiments, actors who disclosed AI use were trusted less than actors who did not. The proposed mechanism is reduced perceived *legitimacy*. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5205850); [RePEc listing](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v188y2025ics0749597825000172.html); [Univ. of Arizona](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/the-transparency-dilemma-how-ai-disclosure-erodes-trust/)
  - The penalty held across disclosure framings, held "above and beyond algorithm aversion," and held **whether disclosure was voluntary or mandatory**. — [ResearchGate abstract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/389852982_The_Transparency_Dilemma_How_AI_Disclosure_Erodes_Trust)
  - **Third-party exposure:** the abstract says the self-disclosure penalty is "comparatively weaker than the effect of third-party exposure." Being exposed by someone else for undisclosed AI use was *more* damaging than disclosing. — [ResearchGate abstract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/389852982_The_Transparency_Dilemma_How_AI_Disclosure_Erodes_Trust); [OBHDP Substack summary](https://obhdp.substack.com/p/new-article-in-obhdp-may-22nd-2025)
  - Moderators: the penalty was *attenuated but not eliminated* among evaluators with favorable attitudes toward technology and those who perceived AI as highly accurate. — [ResearchGate abstract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/389852982_The_Transparency_Dilemma_How_AI_Disclosure_Erodes_Trust)
  - Commentators summarize the "dilemma" this way: most people say AI use should be disclosed, but they hesitate to disclose their own. — [Glessmer blog summary](https://mirjamglessmer.com/2025/06/23/currently-readin-schilke-reimann-2025-on-the-transparency-dilemma-how-ai-disclosure-erodes-trust/); [ASU CISA](https://cisa.asu.edu/AI/AI-trust-and-disclosure)
  - I could not get the full text (NSF PAR host unreachable), so I don't have the per-study Ns, effect sizes, or the exact design of the exposure study. — [NSF PAR record](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10597787)
- **Raj, Berg & Seamans, "The Artificial Intelligence Disclosure Penalty: Humans Persistently Devalue AI-Generated Creative Writing," *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* (2026), DOI 10.1037/xge0001889.** The paper reports 16 preregistered experiments, N = 27,491. Evaluations of creative writing fell when people believed AI was involved, by an average of about 6.2%. Perceived *authenticity* mediated the drop. Interventions did not reliably reduce the penalty: changing story perspective, humanizing the AI, and framing it as human-AI collaboration all failed. The authors caution that the results reflect a period of rapid change and may not last. — [Prolific citation page](https://citations.prolific.com/paper/the-artificial-intelligence-disclosure-penalty-humans-persistently-devalue-ai-generated-creative-wri); [Michigan Ross news](https://michiganross.umich.edu/news/readers-less-favorable-toward-ai-generated-creative-writing-berg-research-finds); [PubMed listing](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41505277/)
  - Note: the "16 studies / 27,000+ participants" figure that circulates on blogs belongs to *this* paper, not Schilke & Reimann. Don't conflate the two.
- **"The AI penalty and disclosure paradox: Trust, authenticity and knowledge uptake in AI-mediated communication" (2026, Elsevier journal via ScienceDirect; N = 547; workplace email and social posts).** Participants said disclosure is important but penalized messages that disclosed AI. The authors warn this creates "perverse incentives for non-disclosure." I did not verify the authors or venue name. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2949882126000551)
- **"Full Disclosure, Less Trust? How the Level of Detail about AI Use in News Writing Affects Readers' Trust" (arXiv 2601.09620, Jan 2026).** One-line disclosures and no disclosure produced similar trust and subscription outcomes. *Detailed* disclosures reduced trust and subscription. This is from a search summary; I could not fetch the sample size. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.09620v1); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/399776774_Full_Disclosure_Less_Trust_How_the_Level_of_Detail_about_AI_Use_in_News_Writing_Affects_Readers'_Trust)
- **Trusting News (US nonprofit) research on AI disclosures in news:** disclosures can both help and hurt trust. Audiences want them, but they lower trust for some readers. — [Trusting News](https://trustingnews.org/new-research-how-ai-disclosures-in-news-help-and-also-hurt-trust-with-audiences/)
- **Oxford/RISJ-affiliated paper "'Or they could just not use it?': The Dilemma of AI Disclosure for Audience Trust in News."** The title captures the audience reaction: disclosure prompts the question of why AI was used at all. — [ORA Oxford PDF](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:65830edf-2b12-41f6-98e3-5855de38dfdd/files/rzw12z6837); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/388526896_Or_They_Could_Just_Not_Use_It_The_Dilemma_of_AI_Disclosure_for_Audience_Trust_in_News)
- **Headline labeling (two preregistered experiments, N = 4,976, reported 2025):** labeling headlines "AI-generated" lowered perceived accuracy and willingness to share, *whether the headline was true or false*. The effect was about three times smaller than labeling a headline "false." This is from a search summary; authors not verified. — [arXiv 2409.03500 cluster / search summary](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.03500)
- **Reuters Institute, *Generative AI and News Report 2025*** (6 countries, ~12,000 adults, June–July 2025): 62% comfortable with news made entirely by humans, 43% with "mostly human with AI help," 21% with "mostly AI with some human oversight." Comfort with human-led news rose slightly year over year. The 2024 Digital News Report baseline (26+ countries): 45% comfortable with mostly-human-plus-AI, 17% with mostly-AI. These figures come via secondary reports; check them against the RISJ PDF. — [journalism.co.uk](https://www.journalism.co.uk/what-the-public-wants-seven-opportunities-for-newsrooms-in-the-age-of-generative-ai/); [Longford Leader / DNR Ireland](https://www.longfordleader.ie/news/national-news/1531529/more-than-half-of-people-uncomfortable-with-news-produced-mostly-by-ai-report.html); [reporterzy.info](https://reporterzy.info/en/5307,journalism-in-the-age-of-ai-why-people-prefer-humans-over-machines.html)
- **Practitioner framing:** Nielsen Norman Group published a "PACED" framework for deciding when to disclose AI use, which treats disclosure as context-dependent rather than universal. — [NN/G](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/disclose-ai-paced/)

### Inferences
- The speaker's claim that "a false 'no AI' claim is worse than silence" is directionally supported. In Schilke & Reimann, third-party exposure of hidden AI use did more damage than self-disclosure. An explicit false denial plausibly adds a *deception* penalty on top of that, but I found no experiment that isolates "false 'no AI' claim then exposed" from "silence then exposed." That specific comparison is inferred, not measured.
- The claim that "trust loss comes from the gap, not from AI use itself" is **only partly supported**. Multiple large studies show that honest disclosure *by itself* reduces trust (Schilke & Reimann), creative evaluations (Raj et al., −6.2%), and perceived headline accuracy. Some of the trust loss *is* about AI use itself. A more defensible version: AI use carries a modest penalty, and the gap between claim and reality carries a larger one.
- The disclosure design matters. A short label seems to cost less than a detailed one (Jan 2026 arXiv news study). That bears on how SlopChop presents provenance.

### Gaps
- No experiment found that directly compares (a) silent AI use exposed, (b) false "no AI" claim exposed, and (c) self-disclosure. That is the exact comparison the speaker's thesis needs.
- Full-text effect sizes for Schilke & Reimann's exposure study were not retrievable.
- Little longitudinal evidence on whether the disclosure penalty shrinks as AI use becomes normal. Raj et al. explicitly flag this as open.

## Q2. AI art and writing aversion: identical work labeled human vs. AI; can people tell; does aversion shrink as quality improves?

### Takeaway
Labeling identical work "AI" reliably lowers ratings, mainly on creativity, meaning, effort, and value, and less on sensory liking and beauty. Lay readers often cannot detect AI text (poetry), and even prefer it when unlabeled. There is little direct evidence that better quality erodes the label penalty. One 2025 study found people conceded equal skill but still discounted AI art heavily on value. That supports the speaker's view that the "quality objection" shrinks while a values or ethics objection remains.

### Cited Findings
- **Bellaiche et al. (2023), "Humans versus AI: whether and why we prefer human-created compared to AI-created artwork," *Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications* (July 2023).** AI-generated images were randomly labeled human-made or AI-made. Study 1 found higher ratings for "human" labels across all criteria (liking, beauty, profundity, worth). In Study 2, perceived *story* and *effort* moderated the label effect for sensory judgments (liking, beauty). Attitudes toward AI shaped communicative judgments (profundity, worth). — [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/c1d43cd30fa3485689a0517e0aa5effe); [Duke Scholars](https://scholars.duke.edu/individual/pub1585977)
- **Millet, Buehler, Du & Kokkoris (2023), "Defending humankind: Anthropocentric bias in the appreciation of AI art," *Computers in Human Behavior* 143, 107707.** Four experiments (N = 1,708), including a high-powered preregistered one. The same artwork was liked less when labeled AI-made, because it was perceived as less creative and induced less awe. The effect was stronger among people who believe creativity is uniquely human. Willingness to pay followed the same pattern. The authors interpret this as defense of an anthropocentric worldview. — [VU Amsterdam research portal](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/defending-humankind-anthropocentric-bias-in-the-appreciation-of-a/); [VU news](https://vu.nl/en/news/2023/artworks-created-by-artificial-intelligence-are-considered-as-less-creative); [UBC news](https://news.ubc.ca/2023/08/people-dislike-ai-art-because-it-threatens-their-humanity/)
- **Porter & Machery (2024), "AI-generated poetry is indistinguishable from human-written poetry and is rated more favorably," *Scientific Reports* 14, 26133 (Nov 2024).** Experiment 1: N = 1,634 US participants, 5 poems by famous poets (Shakespeare, Dickinson, Eliot, Byron, etc.) and 5 ChatGPT-3.5 poems in their styles. Detection accuracy was 46.6%, below chance. AI poems were *more* likely to be judged human-written than real human poems were. Experiment 2: N = 696 rated poems on 14 qualities. When unlabeled, AI poems were rated higher on rhythm and beauty. But being *told* a poem was AI-written lowered ratings regardless of actual authorship. The authors propose that readers mistake the complexity of human poetry for AI incoherence. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11564748); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/aab45fe9151e4db6bd73b968342f706f); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/people-cannot-tell-ai-generated-from-human-written-poetry-and-they-like-ai-poetry-more/)
  - Limitations: non-expert participants. The AI poems imitated the specific poets they were compared against, and the human poems were often difficult canonical verse. — [Scimex](https://www.scimex.org/newsfeed/shakespeare-or-chatgpt-people-prefer-ai-over-real-classic-poetry)
  - The label effect in Experiment 2 (being told "AI" lowers ratings) comes from my recollection of the paper. The search summary did not explicitly confirm it, so verify it in the PMC text.
- **Horton, White & Iyengar (Columbia Business School research brief, June 2025).** Participants conceded that AI art matched human art on skill and nuance but discounted it on creativity, labor, and price. In one exercise, AI-labeled work was valued **62% lower** than human-labeled work. This is a research brief; I did not verify peer-review status. — [Columbia Business School](https://business.columbia.edu/research-brief/digital-future/human-ai-art)
- **de Rooij (2025), "Bias Against Artificial Intelligence in Visual Art: A Meta-Analysis," *Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts* (Nov 2025).** A meta-analysis confirming an anti-AI bias in visual art. Moderator analyses suggest the bias is weaker for representational images. I did not obtain the pooled effect size. — [Tilburg repository PDF](https://repository.tilburguniversity.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/d1cc5a4a-85ce-4aee-9154-09db818cd45f/content)
- **"Bias against AI art can enhance perceptions of human creativity," *Scientific Reports* (2023; authors not verified in this session).** The bias against AI-labeled art persisted even for human–AI collaborations. — [Nature Sci Rep](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-45202-3)
- **Messingschlager & Appel, "Mind ascribed to AI and the appreciation of AI-generated art," *New Media & Society* (online 2023).** The AI label reduced ascribed agency and experience. The authors describe the field's results as "ambiguous." — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14614448231200248)
- A 2024 *AI & Society* paper notes that some studies **failed** to find a bias against AI art, so the literature is not unanimous. — [Springer AI & Society](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-02020-z); see also individual-differences work at [PMC10876601](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10876601)

### Inferences
- The speaker's "quality objection shrinks" claim fits Porter & Machery (unlabeled AI poetry wins on quality) and the Columbia brief (equal skill conceded). The remaining penalty is driven by authenticity, effort, creativity, and anthropocentric beliefs, not by perceived quality. That fits his claim that the objection that persists is about values.
- His "ethics objection grows because better AI art is more substitutable for artists" is a reasonable *economic* argument. But the studies reviewed measure perceived authenticity and creativity, not perceived harm to artists or substitution concerns. I found no study that directly measures ethics or labor-substitution concern rising as quality improves. Some case evidence fits his argument: in the Coca-Cola and Duolingo backlashes, critics emphasized artist and contractor displacement (see Q4).
- Because people cannot tell AI work apart but penalize it when told, provenance depends on *claims*, not *perception*. That is the core rationale for a self-report provenance tool like SlopChop.

### Gaps
- No study found that experimentally varies AI image *quality* while holding the label constant to test whether the penalty shrinks.
- No longitudinal panel tracks AI-label aversion from 2022 to 2026 with the same instrument.
- I did not retrieve the de Rooij meta-analytic pooled effect.

## Q3. Effort heuristic / "signal of care" explanations

### Takeaway
The effort heuristic is well established: the same work is valued more when people believe more effort went into it. Studies of AI art and writing repeatedly identify perceived effort and authenticity as the mechanisms behind the AI penalty.

### Cited Findings
- **Kruger, Wirtz, Van Boven & Altermatt (2004), "The effort heuristic," *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*.** A poem described as taking 18 hours was rated higher in quality and liking, and valued more, than the identical poem described as taking 4 hours. The study also covered paintings and arms and armor. — [Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effort_heuristic)
- **Cho & Schwarz (2008), "Of great art and untalented artists."** The effort-to-quality inference held only when an "effort" lay theory was primed. Priming a "talent" theory weakened it. So the heuristic is malleable. — [U-M Deep Blue](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/items/f5e6e3d7-57bd-483b-adcc-b10d2a62b8be/full)
- **Journal of Business and Psychology (2023, Springer), four experiments, cumulative N = 2,039 (two preregistered).** Evaluators perceive less effort behind AI output, and this lowers creativity ratings. — [Springer s10869-023-09910-x](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10869-023-09910-x)
- Bellaiche et al. (2023): perceived effort moderated the human-vs-AI label effect on liking and beauty. — [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/c1d43cd30fa3485689a0517e0aa5effe)
- Raj, Berg & Seamans (2026): *perceived authenticity* mediates the creative-writing penalty. — [Michigan Ross](https://michiganross.umich.edu/news/readers-less-favorable-toward-ai-generated-creative-writing-berg-research-finds)
- Schilke & Reimann (2025): *perceived legitimacy* mediates the trust penalty. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5205850)
- Arielli (European Society for Aesthetics 2024 abstract) proposes a concept of "artificial effort" in AI art and cites Chamberlain et al. (2018) for lack of perceived effort lowering appreciation. This is a conceptual proposal, not an experiment. — [ESA 2024 abstracts](https://www.eurosa.org/book-of-abstracts-esa-2024/)

### Inferences
- The three proposed mechanisms (effort, authenticity, legitimacy) are distinct but overlapping. All point to audiences valuing evidence of human investment and care. A local newsletter's appeal is especially tied to that signal, which helps explain why a "no AI-bots" claim was a selling point there and why breaking it stings.
- Cho & Schwarz implies the penalty may be reframable. For example, foregrounding the human editorial judgment behind "ai-revised" copy might partly restore perceived effort. This is untested for AI.

### Gaps
- No direct "signal of care" study on newsletters or small local media was found.

## Q4. Cases of undisclosed AI use, false "no AI" claims, and backlash

### Takeaway
The clearest "denial then admission" case is Wizards of the Coast's January 2024 Magic promo. The company publicly insisted the image was human-made, then reversed within days, and the denial became the story. In most other cases (Coca-Cola, Duolingo, Secret Invasion), the AI use was open and the backlash was about the use or labor ethics, not deception. That complicates the "gap, not use" thesis: open use draws backlash too. I could not verify the Lancaster, PA newsletter case.

### Inferences
- **Denial made it worse (WotC, Jan 2024):** the coverage focused on the reversal ("denies, then admits"), and an artist publicly cut ties. This is the strongest real-world analogue to the Lancaster story, and it fits Schilke & Reimann's exposure effect.
- **Open use still triggers backlash (Coca-Cola, Duolingo, Secret Invasion, Late Night):** the objection there was about labor substitution, authenticity, and "slop," not deception. This supports the speaker's "ethics objection" point but undercuts a strong version of "trust loss comes only from the gap."
- **Backlash is not always commercially costly** (Duolingo financials; Coca-Cola repeated the campaign). Reputational effects may be concentrated among creative communities and very online audiences.
- **Unwitting use is common** (WotC vendor, The Onion stock image, the Tor/Bloomsbury stock covers). A "no AI" claim is risky even for honest publishers unless they control their supply chain. That favors scoped claims ("copy written by humans") over blanket ones.

### Gaps
- No public trace of the Lancaster newsletter case.
- Bloomsbury/Maas cover (2024) and Tor/Paolini *Fractal Noise* cover (late 2022) were not verified here. My background understanding: in both, publishers said the AI imagery came from licensed stock (Adobe Stock / a stock site) that was not flagged as AI, and both kept the covers. That is another "unwitting use via supply chain" pattern, but it needs sourcing before use.
- Sports Illustrated (Nov 2023, Futurism's exposé of fake AI author bylines; publisher Arena Group blamed contractor AdVon) was mentioned only in passing in my results; the original reporting was not retrieved.
- No quantitative before/after data (subscriber counts, sales) for any case besides Duolingo's earnings.

## Q5. Platform and institutional disclosure norms

### Takeaway
Disclosure regimes have spread quickly, but they are mostly creator self-report with patchy enforcement. Several distinguish "assisted" from "generated." The EU's Article 50 transparency obligations began 2 August 2026. Technical provenance (C2PA) is gaining capture-side adoption but is routinely stripped by platforms.

### Cited Findings
- **YouTube (March 2024):** creators must disclose *realistic* altered or synthetic content (face or voice swaps, altered real events). Disclosure is not required for clearly unrealistic content, minor edits, or using AI for scripts or captions. Labels go on the player for sensitive topics, otherwise in the description. YouTube may apply labels itself, and it now carries forward C2PA 2.1+ credentials indicating a fully AI-made video. — [YouTube blog](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/disclosing-ai-generated-content); [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/15447836?hl=en); [Storyboard18](https://www.storyboard18.com/brand-marketing/how-youtube-is-enabling-creators-to-disclose-altered-or-synthetic-content-26760.htm)
- **Meta (2024):** the "Made with AI" label was applied to real photos with minor edits (e.g. photographer Pete Souza), triggered by C2PA/IPTC metadata from tools like Adobe. In July 2024 Meta renamed it "AI info." Detection still relies on metadata standards. — [PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2024/06/27/meta-is-evaluating-after-backlash-against-instagrams-made-with-ai-tags); [The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/meta-responds-to-criticism-changes-made-with-ai-label-to-ai-info/); [heise](https://heise.de/-9776887)
- **TikTok:** requires creators to label realistic AI content and shows "AI-generated" labels. Claims about automatic C2PA labeling came only from a marketing blog in my searches, so I did not verify them against a primary source. — [Influencer Marketing Hub](https://influencermarketinghub.com/ai-disclosure)
- **Steam / Valve (policy announced Jan 2024):** developers disclose pre-generated and live-generated AI content in a content survey, shown on store pages. Totally Human's July 2025 analysis found 7,818 of ~114,126 games (~7%) disclosed AI, up from ~1,000 (1.1%) in 2024, and about 1 in 5 new 2025 releases. Visual assets made up 60% of AI-use mentions. A 2026 article notes the disclosure is free-form and not filterable. — [VGC](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/news/steam-games-disclosing-generative-ai-use-are-up-800-this-year/); [mp1st](https://mp1st.com/news/games-released-on-steam-reportedly-used-ai-in-2025); [Stuff.co.za 2026](https://stuff.co.za/2026/02/12/are-video-game-developers-using-ai-players-want-to-know-but-the-rules-are-patchy/); [mysterygamedev analysis](https://mysterygamedev.substack.com/p/steams-new-generative-ai-policy-analysis)
- **Kickstarter (effective 29 Aug 2023):** projects must disclose AI use in a "Use of AI" section, including which parts are original. Improper non-disclosure can lead to suspension. — [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/kickstarter-projects-will-soon-have-to-disclose-any-ai-use-145100394.html); [VGC](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/news/kickstarter-says-new-projects-will-have-to-disclose-whether-they-use-ai)
- **itch.io:** sources conflict. One says disclosure is mandated (especially for asset packs); a later one says there is only an optional searchable "AI Generated" tag. — [Arabian Post](https://thearabianpost.com/itch-io-mandates-generative-ai-disclosure-by-creators/); [Stuff.co.za](https://stuff.co.za/2026/02/12/are-video-game-developers-using-ai-players-want-to-know-but-the-rules-are-patchy/)
- **Spotify (late Sept 2025):** removed 75M+ "spammy" tracks in the prior 12 months, tightened the impersonation (voice-clone) policy, added a spam filter, and backed a **DDEX** industry standard for AI disclosures in credits (supplied by labels and distributors). Spotify says disclosure won't cause down-ranking. Sources differ on whether disclosure is mandatory. — [Music Business Worldwide](https://musicbusinessworldwide.com/spotify-has-deleted-75m-spammy-tracks-as-it-unveils-new-ai-music-policies); [Variety AU](https://au.variety.com/2025/digital/news/spotify-new-ai-safeguards-28228); [Consequence](https://consequence.net/2025/09/spotify-ai-protections)
- **Bandcamp (13 Jan 2026, "Keeping Bandcamp Human"):** bans music generated "wholly or in substantial part" by AI and AI impersonation of artists or styles. Users can report suspected AI, and Bandcamp reserves the right to remove music "on suspicion." "Substantial" is undefined, and AI as a tool within a human process appears to be allowed. — [DJ Mag](https://djmag.com/news/bandcamp-bans-ai-generated-music); [Mixmag](https://mixmag.net/read/bandcamp-has-banned-ai-generated-music-news); [Resident Advisor](https://ra.co/news/84314)
- **Authors Guild "Human Authored" certification:** beta for members launched January 2025, opened to all US-published authors in March 2026 ($10/title for non-members, free for members). It is honor-code based. AI spelling and grammar tools are allowed, as are AI for research, brainstorming, and outlining, but the text must be human-written. Adoption reports conflict: 3,000+ authors / 5,000 titles vs. "over six thousand writers." It has also partnered with UK author societies. — [Publishers Weekly (Mar 2026)](https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/print/20260309/99836-authors-guild-opens-human-authored-certification-to-all-u-s-authors-and-publishers.html); [RightsTech](https://rightstech.com/2026/03/authors-guild-expands-human-authored-certification-program/); [Indie Author Magazine](https://indieauthormagazine.com/us-uk-author-societies-join-forces-to-help-identify-human-authored-works/)
- **EU AI Act Article 50:** transparency obligations applied from **2 August 2026**. These cover disclosing AI interaction, machine-readable marking of synthetic outputs by providers, and deployer labeling of deepfakes and of AI-generated *text published on matters of public interest*. The text-labeling duty is waived if the text underwent human review and is under editorial responsibility. Reported deferrals: 2 Dec 2026 for Art. 50(2) marking of generative systems already on the market; 2 Feb 2027 for watermark-detection interoperability (single source). The Digital Omnibus (Reg. (EU) 2026/1744) delayed high-risk obligations to Dec 2027 / Aug 2028 but reportedly did not amend Art. 50. The Code of Practice on transparency of AI-generated content was finalized around 10 June 2026 and is voluntary. Commission guidelines were published 20 July 2026. Several of these sources are vendor or law-firm blogs, so check them against the Official Journal. — [European Commission Code of Practice page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/code-practice-ai-generated-content); [artificialintelligenceact.eu](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/transparency-rules-article-50/); [Paul Weiss](https://www.paulweiss.com/insights/client-memos/eu-finalises-transparency-rules-for-ai-generated-content); [Jones Day (draft code, Jan 2026)](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/01/european-commission-publishes-draft-code-of-practice-on-ai-labelling-and-transparency); [Technology.org (Oct 2026)](https://www.technology.org/2026/10/13/eu-ai-act-deadlines-after-the-digital-omnibus-what-applies-from-december-2026-to-august-2028/)
- **C2PA / Content Credentials:** capture-side adoption is growing (Google Pixel 10, Aug 2025; Sony Camera Verify, June 2025; Cloudflare Images preservation option). In a Washington Post test (Oct 2025, reporter Kevin Schaul), an AI video with Content Credentials uploaded to eight platforms had its credentials kept or exposed by none. Only YouTube disclosed anything, in the expanded description. Re-encoding, screenshots, and format conversion strip the manifest. C2PA's fallback, "soft bindings" (watermarks or fingerprints), is still maturing. The Post test is reported here via secondary sources. — [top10films summary](https://www.top10films.co.uk/89040-content-authenticity-in-2025-where-c2pa-content-credentials-really-stand/); [SoftwareSeni](https://www.softwareseni.com/durable-content-credentials-how-provenance-survives-metadata-stripping); [ppc.land](https://ppc.land/c2pa/); [ar.io](https://ar.io/articles/missing-layer-content-authenticity/)

### Inferences
- Most regimes (YouTube, Steam, Kickstarter, Spotify/DDEX, Authors Guild) rely on **self-report**, which is SlopChop's model. Several draw an assisted-vs-generated line: YouTube exempts AI scripts and minor edits; the Authors Guild allows grammar tools; Bandcamp targets "wholly or substantially" AI; the EU waives text labels under human editorial review. SlopChop's human / ai-fine / ai-revised taxonomy fits these norms, though category names differ across regimes.
- The EU's "human review + editorial responsibility" exemption for text means a local newsletter in the EU that edits AI-drafted copy might not need a label under Art. 50, even though audiences in the studies above would still penalize it if told.

### Gaps
- No primary TikTok policy page was retrieved.
- itch.io's current policy is unclear (sources conflict).
- No independent data on compliance rates or false-disclosure rates under any self-report regime.

## Q6. AI detection reliability, and audience perception of different kinds of AI assistance

### Takeaway
Text detectors are unreliable and biased. OpenAI withdrew its own classifier, and detectors flag non-native English writing disproportionately. That supports SlopChop's self-report design. On kinds of assistance, the evidence suggests "AI-assisted/edited" labels are penalized less than "AI-generated," but no study cleanly isolates grammar-only editing.

### Cited Findings
- **OpenAI AI Text Classifier, withdrawn 20 July 2023 "due to its low rate of accuracy."** In OpenAI's own evaluation it flagged only 26% of AI-written text as "likely AI-written" and falsely flagged 9% of human text. It was unreliable under 1,000 characters. The withdrawal note was appended quietly to the launch post. — [Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/149826/openai-quietly-shutters-its-ai-text-classifier-due-to-low-accuracy); [Inquirer Tech](https://technology.inquirer.net/126335/openai-shuts-down-ai-detector); [Analytics Vidhya](https://www.analyticsvidhya.com/blog/2023/07/openai-ai-detection-tool-fails-to-detect-74-of-ai-generated-content/)
- **Liang, Yuksekgonul, Mao, Wu & Zou (2023), "GPT detectors are biased against non-native English writers," *Patterns* (Cell Press) / arXiv 2304.02819.** Seven widely used detectors misclassified non-native English (TOEFL) essays as AI-generated about **61%** of the time, while native samples were classified accurately. Simple prompting both mitigated the bias and evaded detection. The authors advise against using detectors in evaluative or educational settings. Turnitin was not tested and disputes generalization to its tool. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02819v1); [Business & Human Rights summary](https://www.business-humanrights.org/en/latest-news/stanford-study-finds-ai-detection-tools-to-be-biased-against-international-students/); [tidymodels fairness case study](https://www.tidymodels.org/learn/work/fairness-detectors/)
- **Image detection:** a study cited in a 2024 arXiv paper (Ha et al. 2024) found that automated detectors and expert artists could often distinguish AI from human images, but each had weaknesses. Lay viewers do much worse. — [arXiv 2408.12762](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.12762)
- **Platform labels misfire:** Meta's metadata-triggered "Made with AI" label hit real photos with minor edits (see Q5). — [PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2024/06/27/meta-is-evaluating-after-backlash-against-instagrams-made-with-ai-tags)
- **Kinds of assistance, label wording (n = 415, between-subjects, news):** "AI-generated" content was rated least credible and "AI-influenced/assisted" most credible. Source trust depended more on editorial value than on the AI label. This is from a search summary; authors not verified. — [arXiv 2409.03500, "Quality Perceptions and Intended Engagement in Response to AI-Generated and AI-Assisted News"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.03500)
- **Swiss preregistered survey (same arXiv 2409.03500 per summary):** found no significant differences in perceived expertise, readability, or credibility across human, AI-assisted, and AI-generated news. Disclosure briefly raised willingness to continue reading. — [arXiv 2409.03500](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.03500)
- **Trust-building messaging study (PMC12765382):** AI writing assistance had little negative effect on trust, even when disclosed. — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12765382/)
- **Scientific writing:** disclosing AI editing did not systematically change reviewer acceptance (arXiv 2511.12529). Researchers avoid disclosing AI assistance, the "transparency paradox." — [arXiv 2511.12529](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.12529); [PMC12515416](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12515416/); [SciELO blog](https://blog.scielo.org/en/2025/10/10/the-transparency-paradox-when-using-generative-ai-in-academic-research/)
- **Reuters Institute 2025:** comfort drops stepwise from human-only (62%) to human-led with AI help (43%) to AI-led with oversight (21%). Audiences distinguish degrees of AI involvement. — [journalism.co.uk](https://www.journalism.co.uk/what-the-public-wants-seven-opportunities-for-newsrooms-in-the-age-of-generative-ai/)
- A reported "Li et al." finding (disclosure lowered AI-assisted essay quality ratings by ~0.32 on a 5-point scale, more for AI-drafted than AI-edited text) could not be traced to a primary source. **Do not cite without verification.**

### Inferences
- The detector evidence makes a strong case for SlopChop's self-report design. A tool that falsely accuses non-native writers or human writers of AI use would itself create the claim-reality "gap" the speaker warns against.
- The graded perception evidence (Reuters Institute tiers; "assisted" rated above "generated") suggests a three-level scheme (human / ai-fine / ai-revised) maps onto real audience distinctions. "ai-fine" (light assistance) is likely penalized least. Labels should be short (cf. one-line vs. detailed disclosure, Q1).
- Self-report's weakness is that it relies on honesty, as the Authors Guild's honor code does. The Raj et al. and Schilke & Reimann penalties give creators an incentive to under-report, the "perverse incentives" noted in the 2026 ScienceDirect paper.

### Gaps
- No study found that isolates *grammar/copyediting only* from substantive AI generation in audience perception.
- No independent 2025–2026 benchmark of current commercial text detectors (GPTZero, Turnitin, Pangram) against frontier models was retrieved in this session.
