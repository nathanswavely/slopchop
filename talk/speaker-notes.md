# Wading Through The Slop: full speaker notes

Archived from the deck before the notes were cut down to scannable bullets. Slides in deck order; the appendix starts after Discussion.

## 1. Wading Through The Slop  `cover`

~0:00–0:15. Quick hello. I’m not here to sell anyone on AI or talk anyone out of it. I want to talk about trust, and what happens when people who build with AI make things for folks who really don’t like AI (which describes a lot of my week). Plan is a local case study from r/lancaster, a rough map of who’s in the room, a bit about consent, a demo of a tool I built called SlopChop, and then I’d rather hear from you.

## 2. Case study: the r/lancaster thread  `shot1`

~1:00–2:00. Be generous here. This is a real person who moved to Lancaster with no jobs and no connections, felt welcomed, and started a free weekly newsletter about local food, music and events to give back. Nothing for sale, and honestly it could be any of us. Then the thread. Someone says the newsletter and the pics “reek of AI,” someone else ties it to gentrification (strip the city of its art and replace it with AI for profit), and the author offers to buy them coffee, “if I show up as AI then you win.” The reply is kind of the whole talk in one line: being a real person isn’t proof you didn’t use AI. He answered “are you a bot?” when the question was “did you use AI?”, and later called it a free grammar editor when the images were generated. Both of those are about the gap between what he said and what he did, more than the AI itself.

## 3. AI disclosure costs trust, but getting caught costs almost twice as much  `research`

~3:00–4:30. The line I’d open with is “the use was forgivable, the claim wasn’t,” but forgivable isn’t free, and the research here is a little uncomfortable. Schilke and Reimann ran 13 experiments, and in every one, people who disclosed AI use were trusted less than people who didn’t. In this one, participants imagined a tax advisor doing their return. Trust sat at 50% (basically neutral, which makes sense for a stranger) when he said nothing, 36% when he disclosed, and 25% when a leak exposed it. So silence that never gets found out scores best, which is pretty much why so many people hide it. The hopeful part is Study 7, where telling people AI use was normal shrank the penalty by about a third, so the cost of being honest depends on the norm and somebody has to go first. If asked: the tax advisor study (Study 13) had 195 people and the “normal” study (Study 7) had 426. It’s one workplace scenario, not a creative community, and “exposed” meant undisclosed use getting caught, not a false “no AI” claim. Trust was four agree/disagree items on a 1–7 scale (raw 4.02, 3.15, 2.49), shown as percent of max, (score − 1) ÷ 6. Wizards of the Coast did the deny-then-admit thing with a Magic promo image in January 2024 (backup slide).

## 4. In the games industry, makers and managers see AI differently  `newline`

~4:30–5:45. We have a rough public playbook for humor, race, religion and politics, and we don’t really have one for AI yet. Part of that is it’s sorting along party lines in real time (Pew: Democrats went from 46% to 56% more concerned than excited since 2023, Republicans from 59% to 49%, backup slide). The other part is this. GDC surveyed 2,300+ people across the games industry, and 52% say generative AI is bad for the industry while 7% say it’s good. Artists (64%), designers (63%) and programmers (59%) are the most negative, and executives and business ops are the most positive at 19%. I expected programmers to be fine with it and they’re not. My guess, and it is a guess, is that working with AI is basically management: you brief it, review it and decide what ships. If you already moved into managing, it lets you make stuff again, and if you chose to be a maker, it turns you into a manager. If asked: GDC only published the “good” number for execs, not the “bad” one. Usage is 30% at studios vs 58% at publishing and marketing firms, and 47% for upper management vs 29% for lower ranks.

## 5. Six Personas  `personas`

~5:45–7:15. Left is against, right is for, and most of us in here are on the far right, which is kind of the point of the slide. The group that actually decides things is in the middle. Refusers are working artists, musicians and writers, and you probably won’t change their minds, but you can keep AI out of their craft and pay them. Quality Skeptics think it’s slop, which is the one objection better work can answer. Wary Pragmatists are the biggest group (Pew has just over a third equally concerned and excited), and all they want is for you not to lie to them, which is exactly who the Loop lost. That middle is shrinking though, GDC’s “mixed” group went from 51% to 30% in a year. Indifferent folks don’t care until a callout tells them what to think. Closeted Users can be anywhere on the line, including people who look like Refusers in public (one GDC respondent: “I’m outspokenly against AI, but then do use ChatGPT as a sounding board for some of my marketing ideas”), and given the research slide, hiding is pretty rational.

## 6. Who was he really talking to?  `reread`

~10:45–11:15. Run the Loop through the persona table.

The author is a Closeted User: uses AI, doesn’t want to say so publicly. They got into an argument with a Refuser, someone from the indie and DIY music scene who opposes AI on principle. No reply was ever going to win that person over.

But the thread had an audience. The Pragmatists and the Indifferent were reading along and voting. The Refuser couldn’t be won, but the Pragmatists could have been kept, and denying it is exactly what loses them.

The audience that mattered never commented. That’s true of almost every public thread you’ll ever be in.

## 7. People can’t hear the difference, but they still want to know that it's AI  `deezer`

~7:15–8:30. Quick line first: as the models get better, “it’s slop” gets harder to say, but the ethics complaint gets stronger. In games, “bad for the industry” went from 18% to 30% to 52% in two years while the tools got way better (backup slide). Deezer had Ipsos survey 9,000 people in 8 countries. In a blind test (three tracks, two of them AI), 97% couldn’t tell which was which, and 80% still want AI music labeled. 73% think training on artists’ work without asking is unethical. So people can’t hear it and still care, which tells me the objection is about who made it and whether they agreed, and that’s the next slide. If asked: three tracks is a pretty small test, and Deezer sells AI detection, so it isn’t neutral, though Ipsos ran the survey.

## 8. We have a playbook for most flashpoints. Not this one.  `playbook`

~6:45–7:30. Like it or not, we’ve more or less negotiated how to present ourselves publicly around humor, race, religion and politics, at least in marketing and public digital spaces. There are always people at the edges who’ll be offended, but there’s a playbook for avoiding needless conflict.

Say this before someone else does: that playbook wasn’t free. It took decades, it’s still contested, and it isn’t evenly respected. The honest claim isn’t “we solved those.” It’s that we have a shared vocabulary and rough maps for those topics. For AI we have neither, and we’re about three years in.

And the biggest difference is on the next slide: the AI line doesn’t follow the divides we already know how to read.

## 9. Three kinds of consent  `consent`

~8:30–10:30. There are three consents in play, which is part of why these conversations get tangled. The source (whose work trained it, and did they agree), the audience (do they know what they’re getting, which is where the Loop went wrong), and the author (is your name on it, since a byline is kind of a promise).

“Consent, credit, compensation” is the creative community’s language, not mine (Karla Ortiz, the Statement on AI Training with 30,000+ signatures, Fairly Trained). What I’d add is why source consent feels broken. Musicians have always been copied, and most of us were fine with that, because you got copied when people heard you and some recognition came back. Training copies everybody whether anyone heard them or not, gives nothing back, and then competes with them.

Code is different, mostly, since open source licenses and Stack Overflow were explicit agreements to share, though even that got stretched (attribution doesn’t get honored, and Stack Overflow users protested the 2024 OpenAI deal).

If someone brings up “fair learning” (Lemley & Casey: copyright never covered style, so this would be a new right), I’d agree with them. I’m talking about the social contract here, and I’m not claiming it’s the law.

## 10. Code has a different "feeling" contract  `codecontract`

~14:00–14:45. Contrast that with code. Generally speaking, programmers have consented to let their code become part of the shared body of human knowledge, through open-source licenses and places like Stack Overflow, whose content is licensed CC BY-SA. Those are explicit, written agreements to share. And most programmers’ identities don’t live in a for loop. Steelman it, because this room will push back, and it should: consent in code isn’t clean either. – Licenses have conditions. MIT requires attribution; GPL requires derivative work to stay open. Models honor neither. The GitHub Copilot class action (Doe v. GitHub, filed November 2022) is about exactly that. – When Stack Overflow announced its OpenAI partnership in 2024, some users tried to edit or delete their answers in protest and got suspended. “I shared this with people” turned out not to mean “I shared this with a model.” Admitting this makes the argument stronger, not weaker. It isn’t “code good, art bad.” It’s a spectrum of how far each community’s original contract was stretched, and art’s was stretched the furthest.

## 11. Some options if your audience doesn’t trust AI  `options`

~10:30–12:00. Personally, I can spend long hours building something actually useful for creatives with AI’s help, and sentiment can still make it close to impossible to get anyone to use it. I don’t think better copy fixes that, so these are some options, and none of them are perfect.

Split “built with AI” from “AI inside,” since a tool written with AI help that generates nothing is a pretty different pitch. Keep AI out of what the audience touches (game devs already do this, one GDC respondent said none of the gen AI in their studio “survives to a stage where players experience it”). Say what it won’t do, like no training on uploads and no generated art.

Build for the Pragmatists and don’t make converting Refusers your growth plan. Let people from the community lead, since a tool launched by a local musician lands differently than one from the AI guy at the coworking space (that’s us). And sometimes the answer is not yet, or not this.

## 12. Copy review for sites built with AI  `slopchop`

~12:00–16:00. Live demo on nathanswavely.com, with this screenshot as backup. Before the talk, run sync in the site repo so review opens fast, and keep the demo to four minutes.

Why I built it: I build a lot with AI, and AI code doesn’t bother me, but AI copy gives me the ick. It’s really hard to go through a site’s copy and fix it without going back to a pre-AI workflow, so this pulls every string a visitor can read, lets me rewrite it or mark it, and keeps a record so I’m not making the same call twice.

Demo: open review, rewrite a sentence from a real paragraph on my site, mark a label “leave as drafted,” explain human / ai-fine / ai-revised, bulk-mark the short labels, then run report and show the numbers.

## 13. Honest categories, not a purity test  `decisions`

~18:00–18:30. The categories are the heart of it, and the honest middle ones matter most.

ai-fine is what makes this bearable. “Failed to load comments” doesn’t need my voice, and neither do most of the labels around it. One keystroke, and it’s recorded as a deliberate choice, not hidden.

ai-revised: if a model suggests a rewrite and I take it without changing a word, that’s what it’s called. It doesn’t get to count as mine.

So “human” only ever means a person typed it. Compare that with the Loop: there were really only two categories there, “AI” and “not AI,” so everything had to be squeezed into “not AI.” More honest categories make honesty easier.

## 14. Most of the words in my own tool aren’t mine  `report`

~16:00–16:45. SlopChop runs on its own repo. Of 83 strings, about 2,000 words, five strings are mine, which is about 9% of the words. One was a model draft I edited, and I looked at the other 77 and left them as drafted on purpose. (If the demo got my site’s numbers, use those instead.)

Compare that to “no AI-bots used in making this lil baby.” It’s a pretty similar situation, a person made something and AI was involved, but one says “no AI” and the other says where and what I chose.

It’s not a detector or a certificate, it’s self-reported. Being a real person isn’t proof you didn’t use AI, and nothing really proves it, so a specific and honest record is about the best you can do.

## 19. Wizards of the Coast did it too, at scale  `wotc`

~5:00–5:30. Quick one; the point is that the Loop isn’t a one-off small-town mistake.

In January 2024, Wizards of the Coast posted a promo image for Magic: The Gathering. Fans pointed out signs of AI. The company publicly insisted the art was made by humans, not AI. A few days later it said it had made a mistake: the image came from a vendor and included AI-generated elements.

Same pattern as the Loop, with a big company and a big fandom: the AI use was the smaller problem; the denial was the bigger one.

Source to check before quoting exact wording: Vice, “Magic: The Gathering publisher denies, then admits using AI art in promo image” (vice.com). Paraphrase rather than quote unless you’ve checked it.

## 20. And now it’s sorting along party lines  `partisan`

~8:30–9:15. The second reason our usual read on a room fails: the partisan map is moving under our feet.

From Pew’s June 2026 survey: 56% of Democrats say they’re more concerned than excited about AI in daily life, up from 46% in 2023. Republicans have gone the other way, down 10 points to 49%. So in 2023, Republicans were the more worried party; now Democrats are.

It’s driven by the ends. Liberal Democrats went from 45% to 63%. Conservative Republicans dropped 14 points. The moderates in both parties haven’t moved significantly.

Same story on jobs: Democrats now expect AI to mean fewer jobs more than Republicans do (75% vs 68%), the reverse of 2024 (58% vs 70%). Overall, about half of Americans are more concerned than excited, up from 37% in 2021.

The line I’d use: “Two years ago this didn’t follow party lines. It’s sorting along them in real time. That’s what happens to an issue before a society has negotiated an etiquette for it. If we don’t set the norms, the culture war will set them for us.”

## 21. One objection fades. The other sharpens.  `qualityethics`

~11:15–12:00. This is the most important slide in this section.

A lot of us in the AI world quietly assume the backlash will fade as the tools get better. That’s true for one objection: the quality one. As the output improves, “it’s slop” gets harder to say. There’s evidence for this half: in a 2024 study (Porter & Machery, Scientific Reports), readers identified AI poems at below-chance rates and rated them higher when they didn’t know.

The ethics objection runs the other way. The better AI art gets, the more it can actually replace artists, so the Refuser’s case gets stronger, not weaker. Be honest that this half is my argument; nobody has measured it directly. But the trend data fit it. GDC’s survey: game professionals calling generative AI bad for the industry went from 18% in 2024 to 30% in 2025 to 52% in 2026, while the tools improved dramatically. And the “mixed” middle collapsed, from 51% to 30% in one year. Their own summary: “It appears that the more game industry professionals know about generative AI, the less they like it.”

So “it’ll be good enough soon” is not a plan for winning over creative communities. And the next slide shows the same thing from the listener’s side.

## 22. Copy sits in the middle  `spectrum`

~14:45–15:15. Beyond consent, there’s a difference in what we’re actually valuing.

Code is judged by what it does. Users never read it, and nobody assumes a person hand-placed every semicolon.

Art is judged partly by who made it. A song or an illustration is a signal that a person paid attention and cared. Take the person out and much of the value goes with it.

Copy, meaning the words on a website or in an app, sits in between. It lives in the codebase, so AI coding tools write it as a side effect. But people read it as a signal from you. That gap is exactly why I built SlopChop.

And notice where the Reddit commenter drew their line: regular word processing and layout software is fine; AI is not. The objection isn’t to tools in general. It’s to the person being taken out.

## 23. Not a detector. Not a certificate.  `notproof`

~19:15–19:45. Be upfront about the limits.

SlopChop isn’t an AI detector and it isn’t a policing tool. It’s self-reported: it records what the reviewer decides. If you want proof for someone else, this isn’t it, and I’m not sure anything is.

Which brings back the commenter from the thread: being a real person is not proof that you aren’t using AI. They’re right. Nothing proves it. What you can offer is a specific, honest record, and then a track record of it being true.

## 24. A disclosure for a hostile audience  `gdcdisclosure`

~20:30–21:00. A second good example, from a real organization rather than my rewrite.

This is from the introduction to GDC’s 2026 State of the Game Industry report, the same survey I quoted earlier. Their audience is game developers, 52% of whom think generative AI is bad for the industry. That’s a hostile room.

Look at what the disclosure does. It says what AI did (sentiment analysis, used sparingly). It says what AI didn’t do (no written or visual content). It flags its one exception and points you to it. And it comes up front, before anyone had to ask. (The exception is a deliberate thought experiment on page 22, where they fed the anonymous survey responses to their internal AI tool and printed its conclusion.)

Compare that with “there’s no AI-bots used in making this lil baby.” Same basic situation: a person or group making something, with some AI involved. One invites a callout. The other is boring, which is the point. A good disclosure should be boring.
