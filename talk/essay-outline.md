# Essay outline: Wading Through The Slop

Outline only. The beats, the evidence for each, and where it came from. The writing is yours.

**Working titles**
- Disclosing costs you, until enough of us do it
- Somebody pays the first cost
- Wading Through The Slop

**The argument in one sentence:** Saying you used AI costs you trust right now, getting caught costs more, and the cost only drops once enough people disclose, so builders who want creative communities to trust them have to go first and pay it.

**Length:** about 2,000–2,500 words. If it runs long, sections 4b and 8 are the ones to shorten, since each can become its own post later.

---

## 1. Open on the Loop

- A newcomer starts a free local newsletter to give back. The PPS says "no AI-bots used."
- The thread: "reeks of AI," then gentrification, then "if I show up as AI then you win."
- The reply that stuck: being a real person isn't proof you didn't use AI.
- He answered "are you a bot?" when he'd been asked "did you use AI?"
- Beat to land: the use was forgivable, the claim wasn't.
- **In print:** anonymize it. No newsletter name, no usernames, no screenshots. Paraphrase the thread.

## 2. The uncomfortable data: honesty isn't free

- Schilke & Reimann 2025, 13 experiments: disclosing lowered trust every time.
- Tax advisor: said nothing 50%, disclosed 36%, caught 25% (% of max on a 1–7 scale).
- Raj, Berg & Seamans 2026: creative writing rated about 6% lower when people believe AI was involved (16 experiments, N = 27,491).
- So silence that's never found out wins, which is why hiding is rational.
- Plant it for later: when AI use was framed as normal, the penalty shrank about a third (Study 7).

## 3. Who's reading

- **No playbook.** Humor, race, religion and politics took decades to negotiate and are still contested, but there's a shared vocabulary. AI has about three years and none.
- **The line doesn't fall where you'd expect.** GDC 2026: makers are the most negative (art 64%, design 63%, programming 59% say bad for the industry), and execs and business staff are the most positive (19% good). Your hypothesis, labeled as one: working with AI is management, so it gives managers making back and turns makers into managers.
- **Party lines are moving under it** (Pew: Democrats 46 → 56% more concerned than excited, Republicans 59 → 49%).
- **Personas.** The Refuser, the Quality Skeptic, the Wary Pragmatist, the Indifferent, the Enthusiast, and the **Closeted User**. The middle decides, and the middle is shrinking (GDC "mixed" 51 → 30% in a year).
- **Re-read the Loop through this.** A Closeted User argued with a Refuser in front of Pragmatists. He couldn't win the Refuser and lost the Pragmatists by denying it, and the audience that mattered never commented.

## 4. What people are actually objecting to

**4a. It's about who made it, not how it sounds**
- Deezer / Ipsos, 9,000 people in 8 countries: 97% couldn't tell AI music in a blind test, 80% still want it labeled, and 73% say training without asking is unethical.

**4b. Three kinds of consent**
- Source, audience, author.
- Credit the borrowed part: "consent, credit, compensation" is the creative community's language (Ortiz, the AI Training Statement, Fairly Trained).
- **Yours:** influence follows reach. Getting copied used to pay you in audience, while training copies everyone, pays nothing, then competes with them.
- **Yours:** the byline as a promise (author consent).
- The code contract as contrast. Code was shared on purpose, but that got stretched too (the Copilot suit, Stack Overflow suspensions). It's a spectrum of how far each contract got stretched, with art stretched furthest.
- Pre-empt "fair learning" (Lemley & Casey). Agree, and say you're describing a social norm, not the law.

## 5. What going first looked like for someone in the room

The Discord story, told with permission and without naming the server.

- He built an efficiency tool for a server he's part of.
- He disclosed precisely: built **with** AI help. It isn't an AI tool, it doesn't generate anything, and it isn't trained on anyone's data.
- There was a significant backlash anyway, and adoption was flat.
- He kept shipping updates, kept talking about it, and kept showing up in the server as a person.
- People came around once they saw it was useful and not slop, and adoption picked up.
- What it shows:
  - **The first-mover cost is real.** Even a precise, honest disclosure got punished at first, which lines up with section 2.
  - **It got paid down.** Usefulness and being present did that over time, not better wording.
  - **It's the options in practice.** He split "built with AI" from "AI inside" and let a community member lead. Only it was him, because he was already a member.
- Get from him if you can:
  - how long it was flat
  - roughly when it turned
  - a number or two (users, installs)
  - one line on what the backlash said

## 6. What builders can do

The options slide, in the same three groups:
- **What's in it:** keep AI out of what they touch. It might cost you efficiency.
- **What you say:** split "built with AI" from "AI inside," and say what it won't do.
  - Short and specific beats long. One study found one-line disclosures did about as well as none, and detailed ones cost trust (arXiv 2601.09620, check before citing).
- **Who you launch with:** build for the Pragmatists, and let the community lead.
- **The way out:** sometimes not yet, or not this.

## 7. Going first myself: SlopChop

- Why: AI code doesn't bother you, but AI copy gives you the ick, and fixing copy meant going back to a pre-AI workflow.
- What it does: sync, review, apply, check. The statuses are human, ai-fine, ai-revised and unreviewed, and "human" only ever means a person typed it.
- Real numbers from nathanswavely.com once you've finished reviewing it, and from SlopChop's own repo (5 of 83 strings yours, about 9% of the words, as of the talk; re-run before quoting).
- Limits up front: it's self-reported, not a detector or a certificate. Nothing proves you didn't use AI, so a specific record is the best you can offer.

## 8. The question the room left open: what happens when it's better?

The Enthusiast's point: at some point AI will be better than people at a lot of things, maybe most, and that changes the social contract. In some technical work it already is, and the public hasn't accepted that.

Threads you could pull (pick one or two, don't resolve it):
- **The quality objection fades, the ethics one gets sharper.** The better it gets, the more it can replace people, so the Refuser's case gets stronger. GDC's "bad for the industry" went 18 → 30 → 52% while the tools improved. Say this half is your argument, since nobody has measured it directly.
- **Better doesn't settle who made it.** Deezer: people can't hear the difference and still want to know.
- **Code already crossed that line, more or less.** That's partly the consent contract, and partly because nobody reads code as a signal from a person. Copy and art are read that way.
- **A possible analogy, check before using:** chess engines have beaten the best humans since 1997, and people still watch humans play. The etiquette that got negotiated was about disclosure (engine prep is fine, an engine during a game is cheating), not about which one is better.
- End on the question: how do we negotiate a contract with something that's inarguably better at the work? That's a good place to ask readers to reply.

## 9. Close: the ask

- Back to the tax advisor. Disclosure costs trust today, and the cost only drops once it's normal, which only happens if people disclose.
- So the first people to do it take the hit for everyone after them. The Discord tool is what paying it looked like, and SlopChop is you paying it.
- The ask: who's willing to go first?
